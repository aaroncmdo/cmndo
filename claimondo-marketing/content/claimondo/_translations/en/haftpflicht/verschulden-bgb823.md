# § 823 BGB – General Fault-Based Liability in Road Traffic Accidents

> **In brief:** § 823 BGB (German Civil Code) is the central **fault-based liability** provision of German law: anyone who **intentionally or negligently** unlawfully injures another person's life, body, health or property is obliged to pay compensation. Unlike § 7 StVG (Road Traffic Act), § 823 BGB requires the **injured party to prove fault**. In road traffic accidents, § 823 BGB applies **alongside** § 7 StVG and § 18 StVG — in practice it is rarely the primary instrument, because § 7 StVG offers the simpler basis for a claim.

## Also known as

§ 823 BGB · Deliktshaftung (tort liability) · Verschuldenshaftung (fault-based liability) · General damages provision · Unerlaubte Handlung (unlawful act)

---

## Explained in one sentence

§ 823 BGB obliges anyone who unlawfully and culpably injures another to pay compensation — the central fault-based liability provision of German law.

## Explained in three sentences

§ 823 Abs. 1 BGB states: "A person who, intentionally or negligently, unlawfully injures the life, body, health, freedom, property or another right of another person is liable to make compensation to the other party for the damage arising from this." In road traffic accidents, § 823 BGB is the third possible basis for a claim **alongside** § 7 StVG (Halterhaftung — keeper liability) and § 18 StVG (Fahrerhaftung — driver liability) — but it requires the **injured party to prove fault** and is therefore rarely the first lever in practice. Its most important function: § 823 BGB applies where the StVG provisions do not — for example where the at-fault party is not operating a motor vehicle (cyclists, pedestrians as wrongdoers) — making it the **gap-filling provision** of the liability system.

## The five key figures

- **§ 823 Abs. 1 BGB** – fault-based liability as a general principle
- **§ 823 Abs. 2 BGB** – breach of a protective statute (e.g. a StVO violation)
- **Intent or negligence** as forms of fault
- **Burden of proof on the injured party** (unlike § 18 StVG)
- **In force since 1900** – cornerstone of German damages law

---

## When § 823 BGB is relevant in road traffic accidents

In practice, § 823 BGB is rarely the leading provision in road traffic accidents — § 7 StVG (keeper liability) and § 18 StVG (driver liability) are more favourable from an evidentiary standpoint. § 823 BGB becomes primarily relevant in the following situations:

| Situation | Why § 823 BGB |
|---|---|
| **Cyclist as at-fault party** | No motor vehicle involved — § 7 StVG does not apply |
| **Pedestrian as at-fault party** | No motor vehicle involved |
| **Animal keeper (e.g. horse on the road)** | § 833 BGB is the specific provision; § 823 BGB serves as a catch-all |
| **Consequential loss suffered by third parties (e.g. shock damage to relatives)** | Extended protective logic |
| **Supplement to StVG provisions** | Where particularly culpable conduct warrants a higher claim |

---

## § 823 Abs. 1 vs. § 823 Abs. 2 BGB

### Paragraph 1 – General provision
Injury to life, body, health, freedom, property or another absolute right — culpably (intentionally or negligently).

### Paragraph 2 – Breach of a protective statute
Violation of a **protective statute** (Schutzgesetz) intended to protect another person. Particularly relevant in road traffic:
- **Criminal Code (StGB)** §§ 142 (hit and run), 222 (negligent homicide), 229 (negligent bodily injury)
- **Road Traffic Regulations (StVO)** – all traffic rules (right of way, speed, following distance)
- **Road Traffic Licensing Regulations (StVZO)** – technical requirements

→ A breach of a protective statute frequently establishes the **element of fault** required by § 823 Abs. 1 — both paragraphs usually operate in parallel.

---

## Intent vs. negligence

### Intent
- The at-fault party knew they would cause harm and intended to do so (rare in traffic)
- Direct intent or conditional intent ("reckless disregard")

### Negligence (§ 276 BGB)
- Failure to observe the **standard of care required in road traffic**
- Simple negligence is sufficient for § 823 BGB

### Gross negligence
- Conspicuously and particularly culpable conduct
- **Does not affect the existence of the claim as such**, but is relevant to the amount of Schmerzensgeld (pain and suffering damages — punitive/satisfaction function) and to comprehensive insurance recourse claims

---

## Burden of proof — the key difference from § 7/§ 18 StVG

| Provision | Burden of proof — fault |
|---|---|
| § 7 StVG | None — strict liability (Gefährdungshaftung) |
| § 18 StVG | Presumed — driver must rebut the presumption |
| **§ 823 BGB** | **Injured party must prove fault positively** |

→ This makes § 823 BGB **harder to use** for the injured party — in straightforward traffic situations, § 7 StVG / § 18 StVG is the faster route. The [Anscheinsbeweis (prima facie evidence)](/haftpflicht/anscheinsbeweis) assists in both cases.

---

## When § 823 BGB is strategically useful

- **Cyclist as at-fault party:** no StVG applies — only § 823 BGB
- **Serious traffic offences:** § 823 Abs. 2 in conjunction with §§ 222/229 StGB as additional leverage
- **Increased Schmerzensgeld** due to gross negligence or intent (satisfaction function)
- **Shock/trauma damage suffered by third parties** — extended protective logic

---

## What to do now — concretely

### 💡 The most important rule
In a standard motor vehicle accident, § 7 StVG is usually sufficient. § 823 BGB is the catch-all provision for special situations or heightened liability.

### 🛠 Two steps

**1. Standard motor vehicle accident** → Direct claim under § 7 StVG / § 115 VVG.
**2. Special situations** (cyclist as at-fault party, animal damage, shock damage to relatives) → Instruct a lawyer and deploy § 823 BGB strategically.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is § 823 BGB?**
The central fault-based liability provision of German law. Anyone who intentionally or negligently injures another person is obliged to pay compensation.

**When is § 823 BGB relevant in a road traffic accident?**
Primarily where the at-fault party is not operating a motor vehicle (cyclists, pedestrians), where conduct is particularly culpable (increased Schmerzensgeld), and in extended consequential loss scenarios (shock damage).

**What is the difference from § 7 StVG?**
§ 7 StVG is strict liability (Gefährdungshaftung) — no proof of fault required. § 823 BGB requires the injured party to positively prove fault.

**What is § 823 Abs. 2 BGB?**
Breach of a protective statute — anyone who violates a protective statute (e.g. StVO, StGB) is liable in damages. Particularly relevant in road traffic, typically in combination with § 823 Abs. 1.

**Which provision gives me better prospects?**
In a standard motor vehicle accident: § 7 StVG (keeper) + § 18 StVG (driver). In special situations, § 823 BGB may apply additionally.
