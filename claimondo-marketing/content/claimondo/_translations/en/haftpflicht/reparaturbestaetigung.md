# Repair Confirmation – Proof of Repair When There Is No Invoice

> **In brief:** A repair confirmation (Reparaturbestätigung) is a brief re-inspection by the expert appraiser: they verify whether the damage calculated in the expert report has been **actually and properly repaired**. You need it whenever a claim depends on the real repair status but there is **no workshop invoice** — in cases of self-repair, loss of use (Nutzungsausfall) despite fictitious billing, and in 130-percent cases.

## Also known as

Reparaturbescheinigung · Reparaturnachweis · Nachbesichtigung · Reparaturabnahme

## In one sentence

It proves to the insurance company that the damage has genuinely been repaired — in situations where an invoice cannot provide that proof.

## In three sentences

With **fictitious billing** (fiktive Abrechnung), you receive the calculated amount as a payout without having to carry out repairs — but the insurance company then has no way of knowing whether your vehicle is still damaged. Several claims hinge on precisely this point: loss of use (Nutzungsausfall) presupposes an actual period of unavailability, and the 130-percent rule requires proper repair plus continued use of the vehicle. The repair confirmation closes this evidentiary gap without requiring you to go through the full process of obtaining a workshop invoice.

## Five key facts

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | a brief re-inspection, not a second full expert report |
| **Who issues it** | the appraiser who assessed the original damage |
| **Legal basis for costs** | § 249 Abs. 2 BGB – costs of pursuing the claim |
| **Typical triggers** | self-repair · loss of use (Nutzungsausfall) · 130-percent case |
| **What it is not** | not a substitute for an expert report; not a TÜV inspection |

## When you actually need it

### Self-repair or repair at an independent workshop without a full invoice

If you repaired the damage yourself, or had it repaired at a workshop that did not issue a complete invoice covering the full calculated scope of work, the insurance company has no documentary evidence at all. The repair confirmation takes the place of that evidence — it does not state what the repair cost, but rather **that the damage has been rectified**.

### Loss of use (Nutzungsausfall) despite fictitious billing

An important distinction applies here — one that tends to get blurred in reduction letters from insurers: you do **not** have to prove that you **would have used** your vehicle. For privately used vehicles, there is a presumption of personal use, and it is the insurer who would need to demonstrate non-use (see [Nutzungsausfall](/haftpflicht/nutzungsausfall)). What is at issue here is something different: the **duration** of the period of unavailability.

When you bill fictitiously, the insurance company sees no repair period and routinely disputes how long the vehicle was actually unavailable. The repair confirmation documents exactly that — not your intention to use the vehicle.

### 130-percent cases

Where repair costs exceed the replacement value (Wiederbeschaffungswert) but remain within the 130-percent threshold, reimbursement is tied to two conditions: **proper repair** and **continued use** of the vehicle. Both must be documented — the repair confirmation is the standard way to do this. For the underlying principles, see [Reparaturkosten](/haftpflicht/reparaturkosten) and [Wiederbeschaffungswert](/haftpflicht/wiederbeschaffungswert).

### When you do *not* need it

- A **complete workshop invoice** covering the full calculated scope of work is already available
- You are billing fictitiously and are **not** making any claims that depend on the repair having taken place
- The damage was purely material, with no loss of use and no 130-percent scenario

## What is assessed — and what is not

The appraiser compares the actual condition of the vehicle against **their own original calculation**. They check whether the items listed in the calculation have been carried out and whether the work was done properly — in other words, whether the vehicle is roadworthy again and the damage has been fully repaired.

| Assessed | Not assessed |
|---|---|
| Has the calculated damage been repaired? | What the repair cost |
| Was the work carried out properly? | Whether the workshop invoiced correctly |
| Is the vehicle roadworthy? | General vehicle condition (not a TÜV substitute) |

> **Why photos are not sufficient:** What is being assessed is the **quality of workmanship**, not the appearance. Whether a structural beam was straightened rather than replaced, whether a weld seam meets the manufacturer's specification, or whether the system was calibrated after suspension work ([ADAS calibration](/haftpflicht/adas-kalibrierung)) — none of this is visible in a smartphone photo. This is why insurers do not generally accept photographs as proof.

## Who issues it

The confirmation should be issued by **the same appraiser who prepared the original expert report**: they know the full extent of the damage and can directly compare the actual state of the vehicle against what was calculated. A different appraiser would first need to familiarise themselves with the prior damage.

Independence is essential — a confirmation issued by the repairing workshop about its own work carries significantly less weight with the insurance company.

## Who bears the costs

The repair confirmation is part of the process of enforcing your claim: it arises because you need to substantiate your entitlement. It is therefore in principle reimbursable by the at-fault party — on the same basis as the costs of the expert report itself, as set out in detail under [Sachverständigen-Kosten](/haftpflicht/sv-kosten).

Typical attempts to reduce or reject costs:

| What the letter says | Why this does not hold up |
|---|---|
| "Costs not reimbursable" | They are incurred because of the insurer's own evidentiary requirements |
| "Re-inspection not necessary" | Without it, the same insurer denies the loss-of-use claim |
| "Repair method not evidenced" | That is precisely what the confirmation provides — it is the answer, not the problem |

## Concrete next steps

1. **Clarify before the repair** whether you intend to claim anything that depends on it — loss of use (Nutzungsausfall) or the 130-percent rule. If so, plan for a re-inspection appointment.
2. **Keep everything:** spare parts receipts, photos taken during the repair, workshop notes. They assist the appraiser, even if they do not replace the inspection.
3. **Instruct the same appraiser** who prepared the original expert report.
4. **Submit the confirmation together with your claim statement** — do not wait to provide it on request. This shortens the settlement process.

### 💡 The key point

The repair confirmation is **not an additional burden imposed on you** — it is the direct response to an evidentiary requirement that the insurance company itself imposes. Having it removes the basis for the most common reduction argument in fictitious billing cases.
