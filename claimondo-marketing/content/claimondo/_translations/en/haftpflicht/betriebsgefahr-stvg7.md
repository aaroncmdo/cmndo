# Operational Risk under § 7 StVG – Keeper Liability Without Fault

> **In brief:** § 7 StVG (German Road Traffic Act) is the central **strict liability** provision for vehicle keepers: anyone who keeps a motor vehicle is liable for damage arising from its operation – **even without personal fault**. The operational risk alone (a motorised vehicle in traffic) is sufficient. The liability insurer steps in directly for the keeper under § 115 VVG (Insurance Contracts Act). The only exception is **force majeure** (§ 7 para. 2 StVG) – practically very rarely successful (wildlife collision, animal, a fully unavoidable event).

## Also Known As

Keeper liability · strict liability for motor vehicles · § 7 StVG liability · operational risk

---

## Explained in One Sentence

§ 7 StVG is the fault-independent liability of the vehicle keeper for all damage arising from the operation of their vehicle – without them having done anything "wrong."

## Explained in Three Sentences

The operational risk under § 7 StVG (Betriebsgefahr) is the **core provision** of Germany's motor vehicle liability system – it obliges every keeper of a motor vehicle to pay compensation as soon as a person is injured, killed, or property is damaged during the use of their vehicle. **Unlike § 823 BGB** (German Civil Code), § 7 StVG requires **no fault** on the part of the keeper – the mere act of putting the potentially dangerous vehicle into operation (strict liability / Gefährdungshaftung) is sufficient; the liability insurer steps in directly under § 115 VVG. Routes to discharge are narrow: **force majeure** (§ 7 para. 2 StVG – a falling tree, an animal jumping into the road, vis maior) and the **unavoidable-event defence** (rarely successful) – in practice § 7 StVG applies in almost all road accident scenarios.

## The Five Key Figures

- **§ 7 StVG** – the anchor provision of German motor vehicle liability
- **§ 115 VVG** – direct claim against the liability insurer
- **Fault-independent** – no proof of "blame" required
- **Force majeure** as the only significant exception (§ 7 para. 2)
- **§ 18 StVG** – running in parallel: driver liability with presumed fault

---

## What Operational Risk Means in Law

A motor vehicle on public roads is, under German legal doctrine, a **permitted but particularly dangerous** activity. When the legislator introduced what was then the new Road Traffic Act in 1909, it permitted participation in traffic by private car but imposed **extended liability** on every keeper: even if they behave entirely correctly, they bear the risk that damage arises from the operation of their vehicle.

### Three Requirements
1. **Motor vehicle** within the meaning of the StVG (car, truck, motorcycle, e-scooter exceeding 6 km/h)
2. **"During operation"** – the damage has a **functional connection** with the vehicle's movement or stationary position (including when stationary with the engine running, during parking manoeuvres, when opening a door)
3. **Injury to life, limb, health, or property**

→ Merely keeping and operating the vehicle **without** personal fault is sufficient to trigger liability – that is the essence of strict liability (Gefährdungshaftung).

---

## Keeper vs. Driver – Who Is Liable and When

| Role | Liability Basis | Fault Required? |
|---|---|---|
| **Keeper** | § 7 StVG | No – strict liability (Gefährdungshaftung) |
| **Driver** | § 18 StVG | Yes, but **presumed** (see [Driver liability](/haftpflicht/fahrerhaftung-stvg18)) |
| **Owner acting as keeper** | § 7 StVG | No |

The **keeper** is the person who **operates the vehicle at their own expense**, has control over it, and bears its costs – not necessarily the owner (e.g. the lessee under a leasing agreement is the keeper).

---

## Force Majeure – the Narrow Exception

§ 7 para. 2 StVG permits discharge from keeper liability only in cases of **force majeure** – defined as an event "coming from outside, extraordinary in nature, and impossible to avert."

### Practical Examples
- **Natural disaster** (earthquake, storm surge, lightning strike directly hitting the vehicle)
- **Wildlife collision** (involving a registration-required animal in an unforeseeable situation – often contested)
- **Suddenly falling tree** due to a storm

### What Does **Not** Constitute Force Majeure
- Black ice (part of the ordinary risk of road traffic)
- Oil spill on the road (the keeper of the other vehicle may be liable, but operational risk persists)
- Sudden defect in one's own vehicle (tyre blowout, brake failure – more likely argued as an unavoidable event)

---

## Relationship to the Direct Claim

§ 7 StVG establishes the **keeper's liability** – but under **§ 115 VVG** the injured party has their own **direct claim** against the keeper's **liability insurer**. Practical significance: you do not have to sue the keeper first; you can communicate directly with the insurer.

## Burden of Proof and Prima Facie Evidence

Under § 7 StVG the evidentiary position for you as the injured party is very favourable:

- You prove: **accident + damage + causal link to the operation of the vehicle**
- The insurer proves: **force majeure or contributory negligence** (see [§ 17 StVG contributory liability](/haftpflicht/mitverschulden-stvg17))
- In typical road accident scenarios, the [prima facie evidence rule (Anscheinsbeweis)](/haftpflicht/anscheinsbeweis) applies additionally in your favour

---

## What to Do Now – Concretely

### 💡 The Most Important Rule
You do **not** have to prove any fault on the part of the opposing keeper. It is sufficient that their vehicle was involved in the accident "during operation."

### 🛠 Three Steps

**1. Identify the keeper**
Registration certificate / keeper enquiry with the Kraftfahrt-Bundesamt (Federal Motor Transport Authority). The keeper is usually listed in the police report.

**2. Assert the direct claim against the insurer**
§ 115 VVG – you communicate directly with the keeper's liability insurer.

**3. If the insurer invokes force majeure: instruct a lawyer**
The force majeure argument rarely succeeds – if the insurer raises it, engage a lawyer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is operational risk (Betriebsgefahr)?**
The risk arising from the operation of a motor vehicle. § 7 StVG obliges the keeper to pay compensation for all damage arising from the operation of their vehicle – even without personal fault.

**Is the keeper liable without fault?**
Yes. § 7 StVG is strict liability (Gefährdungshaftung) – the mere act of putting the potentially dangerous vehicle into operation is sufficient. Discharge is possible only in cases of force majeure (§ 7 para. 2 StVG).

**What is the difference from § 823 BGB?**
§ 823 BGB requires **fault** on the part of the person causing the harm; § 7 StVG does not. Both can apply concurrently – § 7 StVG is usually the more easily proven basis.

**What counts as "force majeure"?**
Very rarely met and narrowly defined: natural disaster, a tree suddenly felled by a storm. Black ice, an oil spill, or a tyre blowout typically do **not** qualify.

**Who is the keeper of a leased vehicle?**
The lessee – not the leasing company. Whoever operates the vehicle at their own expense and bears its costs.

**Can I communicate directly with the insurer?**
Yes. § 115 VVG gives you the direct claim – you do not have to sue the keeper.
